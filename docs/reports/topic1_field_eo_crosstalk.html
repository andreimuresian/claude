How the EO crosstalk is extracted from the CST fields

Topic 1 of 4 · from an electric field in two gaps to one number: how much modulator B moves when only A is driven

The idea in three sentences CST gives us the electric field along both lines. From it we read the lateral field in every gap, where the light runs, at every position along the line. Then we "fly" the light of each modulator through those fields, exactly as the real light would, and compare what modulator B picks up with what modulator A picks up. No circuit model and no fitting is involved: only physics that holds for this geometry.

0. The question

Two Mach–Zehnder modulators (MZMs) sit side by side, 625 µm apart. Each one has its own electrode line, a coplanar waveguide (CPW): a central signal strip between two ground strips, with a 4.15 µm gap on each side. The two optical arms of each MZM run under its two gaps. We drive line A only. Some of its field leaks onto line B, so modulator B modulates a little too, although nobody drives it. The EO crosstalk is

\[ X_{EO}(f) \;=\; \frac{\text{optical modulation produced in MZM B}}{\text{optical modulation produced in MZM A}} \qquad (\text{reported as } 20\log_{10}|X_{EO}| \text{ dB}). \]

Both modulations are small signals at frequency \(f\), so \(X_{EO}\) is a ratio of two complex amplitudes. The rest of this report builds the numerator and the denominator in four steps.

Step 1Which field the light feels, and what we take from CST

Coordinates. \(z\) runs along the line (0 to 8 mm). \(y\) is lateral, across ground, gap, signal, gap, ground. \(x\) is vertical: \(x=0\) is the top of the LN slab, and the 2 µm gold spans \(0 < x < 2\) µm.

What the light responds to. The LN is X-cut: its crystal axis (the "optical axis" \(c\)) lies in the plane, along \(y\). The light is a TE mode, polarised along \(y\) too. Its refractive index then changes through the largest electro-optic coefficient, \(r_{33}\), in proportion to the lateral field (Pockels effect):

\[ \Delta n_y = -\tfrac12\, n_e^3\, r_{33}\, E_y . \]

The other field components couple only through much smaller coefficients. At our sample points \(|E_x/E_y|\) is 0.02 (median) and \(|E_z/E_y|\) is below 0.001 (median), so we ignore them. In the device, the light is guided by a rib 1 µm wide and 200 nm tall, centred in each gap, on top of the slab (\(0 < x < 0.2\) µm). The quantity we need is therefore \(E_y\) at the rib, at every \(z\).

What we export. CST gives \(E_y\) at one point per gap: the gap centre (within 0.4 µm) at mid-metal height, \(x_0=1\) µm, every 5 µm along the 8 mm. That is 1601 points per gap. Each one is used individually in the integral of Step 2, with no averaging. Define the two gap fields of a line, each counted positive when it points from the signal towards its ground:

\[ E_+(z) = E_y(x_0, y_{+}, z), \qquad E_-(z) = -E_y(x_0, y_{-}, z), \]

where \(y_\pm\) are the centres of the + side and − side gaps. The sign on \(E_-\) is there because "towards the ground" is \(-y\) in that gap.

Why one point in the air gap can stand for the field at the rib The simulated model has no rib (it was left out to save computation). So in the simulation, the rib region is vacuum between the same two gold sidewalls as our sample point, 0.9 µm lower. The gap is 4.15 µm wide and the wavelength on the line is about 4.5 mm at 30 GHz, 1000 times longer. Across the gap, the field therefore has the shape of a static field between two electrodes, scaled by the drive. Two points in the same cross-section then keep a fixed ratio: \[ E_y^{\,\mathrm{rib}}(z) = r(z)\; E_y(x_0, y_\pm, z). \] Analogy: a rain gauge on the roof instead of in the garden. It reads a different amount, but in a fixed proportion, so it tells the same story. Near a chimney the proportion may change.

The tees make the field periodic, and that is real. Every 180 µm, the base of a T slot recessed into the ground comes close to the gap. There the field in the gap is smaller: on line A it falls to 0.44 of its value elsewhere, at every frequency (the dips in Fig. 2). The rib at those positions feels the same reduction, so the dips belong in the result, and they are in it: each of the 1601 points enters the integral with its true value. Line B shows a different, frequency-dependent shape at the stems, sometimes even a peak. B carries a mixture of waves (Step 2), and each wave has its own local field near the stem. That is physical too, and the light would feel it the same way.

What cancels in the ratio B/A, and what does not Only a factor that is the same at every \(z\) and on both lines can be taken out of the two integrals of Step 4 and cancelled. The material constants \(n_e\), \(r_{33}\), the optical wavelength and the mode overlap are such factors. A factor that varies along \(z\) cannot be taken out, so nothing of that kind is divided away. The periodic field is not a factor to remove; it is part of the field we integrate.

The one factor that must cancel is the sample-to-rib ratio \(r\). If it is constant, using \(E_y(x_0)\) instead of \(E_y^{\,\mathrm{rib}}\) changes nothing in \(X_{EO}\):
  • Flat part of each cell (about 75 % of the length): the cross-section is a uniform gap, so \(r\) is constant.
  • Stem region (about 40 µm of each 180 µm cell): not verified. The geometry changes along \(z\) there, so the field at \(x_0=1\) µm and at the rib height may not dip in exactly the same proportion.
To see how much rides on it, we recomputed \(X_{EO}\) with the stem points of every cell removed from both lines. The result moves by 0.5–1.1 dB from 25 to 60 GHz, and by 1.7–4.5 dB from 5 to 20 GHz. That is the weight of the stems in the result, not the error: if \(r\) is even roughly constant there, the error is a small fraction of it. Exporting the same 24 files at \(x=0.1\) µm (rib mid-height) would measure it directly.

A note on voltage. The usual way to describe a line is its voltage \(V(z)\): the potential difference between signal and ground in the cross-section at \(z\). It is obtained by integrating the field along a path that crosses the gap at fixed \(z\), from gold to gold:

\[ V(z) = \varphi_{\mathrm{sig}}(z) - \varphi_{\mathrm{gnd}}(z) = \int_{\mathrm{signal}}^{\mathrm{ground}} \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_{y_{\mathrm{sig}}}^{y_{\mathrm{gnd}}} E_y(x_0,y,z)\,dy . \]

Here \(z\) only labels the cross-section; the integral runs across \(y\). On a straight path at fixed \(x_0\), \(d\mathbf{l}=\hat y\,dy\), so only \(E_y\) enters. Any other gold-to-gold path in the same cross-section gives the same \(V\), up to \(-j\omega\mu_0\iint H_z\,dA\) over the few µm\(^2\) between the paths, which is negligible for this quasi-TEM line. A path inside the slab, from LN to LN, does not reach the gold, so it would give only part of \(V\). The integral along \(z\) is a different thing: it is what the light does in Step 2. We do not need \(V\) at all: the light responds to the field, so the method works with \(E_+\) and \(E_-\) directly. On the flat part of a cell, \(E_\pm\) is simply proportional to \(V\). At the stems, the local field changes shape, so it is no longer.

The missing rib. A real LN rib (relative permittivity about 28 along \(y\)) would reduce and reshape the field inside it. This is a choice made in building the model, not something an export can recover. Our result is exact for the model as built.

Step 2From the field to the optical phase: the light rides the wave

Pockels effect. By Step 1, the index of the optical arm changes in proportion to the gap field. A short piece \(dz\) of optical arm therefore adds a phase

\[ d\varphi = \kappa\, E(z,t)\, dz , \]

where \(E\) is the gap field of Step 1 and \(\kappa\) collects \(n_e^3 r_{33}\), the optical wavelength and the overlap between optical mode and electric field. It is the same at every \(z\) and for both modulators, so it cancels in the end.

The light is moving. A slice of light that enters at time \(t_0\) reaches position \(z\) at time \(t_0 + z/v_o\), with \(v_o = c/n_g\) the optical group velocity. (It's the group index, because a modulation travels with the envelope of the light, not with its phase fronts.) Summing all pieces along the arm of length \(L\):

\[ \varphi(t_0) = \kappa \int_0^L E\!\left(z,\, t_0 + \tfrac{z}{v_o}\right) dz . \]

Phasors: what \(\tilde E\) is. The drive is a sinusoid at angular frequency \(\omega = 2\pi f\). At a fixed position \(z\), the field therefore oscillates as a cosine, with its own amplitude \(A(z)\) and its own phase \(\theta(z)\): \[ E(z,t) = A(z)\cos\!\big(\omega t + \theta(z)\big) = \mathrm{Re}\big\{\tilde E(z)\, e^{j\omega t}\big\}, \qquad \tilde E(z) = A(z)\, e^{j\theta(z)} . \] The phasor \(\tilde E(z)\) is one complex number per position: its modulus is the amplitude, its angle is the phase. It is exactly what a CST frequency monitor stores and exports (the columns EyRe, EyIm are \(\mathrm{Re}\,\tilde E_y\) and \(\mathrm{Im}\,\tilde E_y\)). The identity uses \(e^{j\alpha} = \cos\alpha + j\sin\alpha\).

Substituting into the phase. The light that enters at \(t_0\) meets the field at time \(t_0 + z/v_o\): \[ E\!\left(z, t_0 + \tfrac{z}{v_o}\right) = \mathrm{Re}\big\{\tilde E(z)\, e^{j\omega t_0}\, e^{j\omega z/v_o}\big\} . \] Both "take the real part" and "integrate over \(z\)" are linear, so their order can be swapped, and \(e^{j\omega t_0}\) does not depend on \(z\), so it comes out of the integral: \[ \varphi(t_0) = \kappa\, \mathrm{Re}\Big\{ e^{j\omega t_0} \int_0^L \tilde E(z)\, e^{j\beta_o z}\, dz \Big\}, \qquad \beta_o = \frac{\omega}{v_o} = \frac{\omega n_g}{c} . \] This has the same form as the first equation: the optical phase is itself a sinusoid in time, and its phasor is \(\kappa \int_0^L \tilde E(z)\, e^{j\beta_o z} dz\).

Two cosmetic choices give \(H\). (1) Time is counted at the moment the light leaves the arm, \(t_e = t_0 + L/v_o\), so \(e^{j\omega t_0} = e^{j\omega t_e}\, e^{-j\beta_o L}\); this moves a factor \(e^{-j\beta_o L}\) into the integral. (2) The integral is divided by \(L\), so that the result is a field (V/m), an average over the arm. Then \(\varphi(t_e) = \kappa L\, \mathrm{Re}\{ H\, e^{j\omega t_e}\}\), with

\[ \boxed{\,H[\tilde E] = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^L \tilde E(z)\, e^{j\beta_o (z - L)}\, dz\,} \]

Neither choice affects \(X_{EO}\): both multiply A and B by the same factor. \(H\) is the effective field the light experiences. Its meaning shows in the reference case of a lossless wave travelling with the light, \(\tilde E(z) = E_0 e^{-j\beta_o z}\) (amplitude \(E_0\), phase falling along \(z\) at the light's own rate): the exponentials cancel and \(H = E_0 e^{-j\beta_o L}\), so \(|H| = E_0\). The light rides one crest and collects its full amplitude along the whole arm. (At DC, \(\omega = 0\), a uniform field \(E_0\) gives \(H = E_0\) as well.)

Metaphor: the surfer A wave travelling forward along the electrode gives \(\tilde E = E_0 e^{-j\beta z}\). In the integral it multiplies \(e^{+j\beta_o z}\). If the wave moves as fast as the light (\(\beta = \beta_o\)), the two phases cancel: the light surfs the same crest all the way and collects the full field at every step (blue line in Fig. 3). If the wave is slower, the surfer slowly slides off the crest, and what it collects stops growing (green). A wave travelling backward meets the light head-on. The light crosses crest, trough, crest, trough, and the contributions cancel. It's like walking against a crowd: you brush past many people, but none stays with you (orange). For our lines, a backward wave contributes only \(\approx 2/\big((\beta_o+\beta)L\big) = 0.09\) (−21 dB) of what a matched forward wave of equal size gives.

Step 3Two arms, push-pull: the modulator reads only one pattern

An MZM splits the light into two arms and recombines it. Biased at quadrature, its output intensity changes in proportion to the difference of the two arm phases:

\[ \Delta P_{out} \propto \varphi_1 - \varphi_2 . \]

Arm 1 sits under the + side gap, arm 2 under the − side gap. \(E_+\) and \(E_-\) are the two gap fields of Step 1, each counted from the signal towards its ground. When both are positive, \(E_y\) points in opposite directions in the two gaps (Fig. 1). The crystal axis is the same for both arms, so the two arms get opposite phase shifts: \(\varphi_1 = +\kappa H[E_+]\) and \(\varphi_2 = -\kappa H[E_-]\). Then

\[ \Delta P_{out} \propto \kappa\,\big(H[E_+] + H[E_-]\big) = \kappa\, H[E_+ + E_-] . \]

Every field pattern on a line can be split into two parts:

PatternGap fieldsField in the two gapsEffect on the push-pull MZM
CPW (normal drive)\(E_+ = E_-\)opposite directionsadds up: full modulation
Slot (grounds at opposite potentials)\(E_+ = -E_-\)same directioncancels: no modulation

Analogy: a seesaw. Pushing one end down and lifting the other (CPW) tilts it. Lifting both ends together (slot) moves it up but doesn't tilt it, and the MZM only measures tilt.

Step 4The ratio

Applying Steps 1–3 to each modulator, the constants (\(\kappa\), bias, optical power) are the same at every \(z\) and for A and B, so they cancel and the ratio keeps only the gap fields:

\[ \boxed{\,X_{EO}(f) = \frac{H\big[E_{B+} + E_{B-}\big]}{H\big[E_{A+} + E_{A-}\big]}\,} \]

Each \(E\) is read from CST at the sample point of its gap, at all 1601 points along the 8 mm, and enters with its own value: the dips at the tees included. The integrals are evaluated on the export grid, every 5 µm (36 points per 180 µm cell), with the trapezoidal rule. (The code also allows unequal arms, a push-pull imbalance; with identical arms it reduces to this formula.)

At 30 GHz on CORDOBA, the victim's largest gap field is −52 dB relative to A, yet \(X_{EO} = -76\) dB. The difference is Step 2 at work: much of the victim's field isn't phase-matched to the light (backward waves, waves at other speeds), so the light averages it away.

Where \(\tilde E(z)\) comes from, and the conditions behind it

CST excites port 1 (line A, near end) with all four ports terminated in 50 Ω, and records the field. If the real source and load are also 50 Ω, that field is the device case, and nothing else is needed. For other terminations we use linearity: the field in any situation is a sum of the four single-port fields, each weighted by the wave entering that port, \(\tilde E = \sum_j a_j F_j(z)\). The weights \(a_j\) follow from the S-matrix and the reflection of each termination. The fields of ports 2–4 follow from port 1 by symmetry (mirror A↔B, reversal near↔far end).

What this number is, and what it is not

It is the exact EO crosstalk of the simulated structure, at the monitor frequencies. Exact here means no circuit model and no fitted parameter, only the physics above.
It is not (1) a curve: we only have points every 5 GHz; (2) the chip itself: the simulation has an open bottom instead of the conductive backside, and its lateral boundary sits 21 µm from the structure, suspected of producing the extra n ≈ 2.5 wave (the widened 625 µm run tests this); (3) transferable to another length. Filling the gaps between frequencies and changing the length is the job of a mode model (topics 3 and 4), and this report's numbers are what such a model must reproduce.
