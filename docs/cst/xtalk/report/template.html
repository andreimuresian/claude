1. Summary for the meeting

  • The inputs come from 3-D, 2-D and eigenmode CST runs. Your coworker's 3-D time-domain runs of the 200 µm pair (1 mm and 5 mm) give the average line: loss, index and impedance. The small difference between the even and odd modes comes from our 2-D port-mode solve (frequency dependence of \(\Delta n\)), corrected for gold with the eigenmode solver: the 2-D solve uses PEC metal, and gold raises \(\Delta n\) by about 15 %. The impedance split \(\delta=Z_e/Z_o-1\) is 0.21–0.61 %, depending on the impedance definition (§3.4).
  • The model is exact for a uniform pair: two identical coupled lines behave as two independent single lines, the even mode and the odd mode. Each one is our single-line travelling-wave element with its own table, and the two are recombined at the ports. No segmentation is involved, and every quantity, including the voltage integral the light sees, is closed-form.
  • The length dependence comes from the physics, not from fitting. \(L\) enters only through \(e^{-\gamma_{e,o}L}\). Far-end crosstalk grows roughly as \(\pi f L\,\Delta n/c\) (minus the line loss). Near-end crosstalk saturates at about \(\delta/2\) and has nulls every \(c/(2nL)\).
  • The shaded band is not a probability. It is the envelope between two runs of the model, with \(\delta=0.21\,\%\) and \(\delta=0.61\,\%\): the spread of the impedance split across the impedance definitions that fit a GSG launch, from the gold eigenmode run (§3.4).
  • The twin and INTERCONNECT agree. The element's code matches the twin to about \(10^{-15}\), and in INTERCONNECT the aggressor matches to ≤ 0.09 dB and ≤ 0.44°, with the crosstalk error 72–84 dB below the aggressor.
  • The PAIR_* blocks are a test bench, not the product. The product is the single scripted element "Coupled TL". Putting it into the full MZM cascade is the logical next step: it is mechanically easy, and it was paused only because we agreed not to touch the MZM code yet.

3. From CST to model inputs: which simulations, and why

3.1 The average line: the 3-D runs

Your coworker's two 3-D pair runs (1 mm and 5 mm) are combined into the even and odd 2-port responses, then averaged. The ratio of the two lengths' transfer matrices cancels the port transitions, because the ports are identical in both runs. Its eigenvalues are \(e^{\pm\gamma\,\Delta L}\) with \(\Delta L = 4\) mm, which gives \(\gamma(f)=\alpha+j\beta\) without any port model. \(Z(f)\) then follows from a small port model. The result agrees with the separate single-line 3-D runs to within 0.6 % in index and 3.6 % in impedance:

3.2 Why not take the crosstalk straight from the 3-D runs?

We tried, and it failed for two reasons:

  1. Numerical error floor. Lines A and B are identical, so \(S_{21}\) must equal \(S_{43}\). Their difference shows the run's own numerical error: −73 dB at 1 mm, but −56 dB at 5 mm, where the 5 mm crosstalk (about −57 dB) sits.
  2. Chaining 1 mm blocks fails. Cascading five 1 mm 4-ports reproduces the through path, but not the crosstalk: −79 dB chained against −53 dB direct at 30 GHz. The 1 mm crosstalk is too close to that run's floor, and port effects are counted at every junction.

So the crosstalk can't be read off the 3-D data reliably. It has to come from a quantity that is computed cleanly, which is the even/odd split.

3.3 The split: the 2-D port-mode solve

One waveguide port covering both lines, solved with "port modes only" at 6, 18, 30, 42 and 60 GHz. With 6 metal strips and the metal frame there are 7 conductors, hence exactly 6 quasi-TEM modes, which is what CST found:

Why only modes 5 and 6 matter. A GSG launch, such as a probe or our bridge port, ties a line's two grounds together and returns the signal current on that line's own grounds. That gives zero amplitude to the slot modes 3 and 4 (opposite ground potentials) and to modes 1 and 2 (net current through the frame). What's left is "line A in its coplanar mode" and "line B in its coplanar mode", and those are exactly the even and odd combinations:

\[ \mathbf v_{A} = (1,0) = \tfrac12(1,1) + \tfrac12(1,-1) = \tfrac12\,\mathbf v_{\rm even} + \tfrac12\,\mathbf v_{\rm odd}. \]
  • Index split from the solver's β values: \(\Delta n = (\beta_5-\beta_6)\,c/(2\pi f) = 0.00150\) (6 GHz) to \(0.00157\) (60 GHz).
  • Impedance split from the exported fields of modes 5 and 6. Integrating \(E_y\) across the strips gives each strip's potential, so each line's voltage is \(V_A=V_{S_A}-\tfrac12(V_{G_{A,l}}+V_{G_{A,r}})\). With both modes at the same power, \(Z_e/Z_o=(V_{A,e}^2+V_{B,e}^2)/(V_{A,o}^2+V_{B,o}^2)\). From that single run per frequency, this gives \(\delta=0.30\) to \(0.45\,\%\), depending on which grid row through the 2 µm metal is used: the field peaks sharply at the metal edges, so the post-processing is sensitive to where it's done. That was the first band; §3.4 replaces it.

Why the 2-D solve isn't used for the absolute line, and why its split needed correcting

The port solver treats the lossy gold as PEC (perfect conductor), so its index is 1.888, against 1.95 from 3-D, and it has no loss. The first version of this report claimed both modes are affected equally, so the difference survives. That is only true to first order: the gold eigenmode run (§3.4) shows gold raises Δn by about 15–20 %, because the even and odd current distributions differ slightly and so does their internal inductance. The model now corrects for it.

3.4 Correction: gold instead of PEC, and the impedance definition

The eigenmode solver (one 10 µm cell of the pair, periodic boundaries, even and odd via a symmetry plane) was run with PEC and with gold on the same line, at about 29 GHz:

  • Index split: the PEC eigenmode run reproduces the 2-D value within 5 %, so the 2-D method is consistent. Gold raises it to 0.00173. The model keeps the 2-D frequency dependence and scales it by \(0.001731/0.001511=1.146\), so Δn ≈ 0.0017–0.0018 over the band. Far-end crosstalk rises by \(20\log_{10}1.146\approx1.2\) dB.
  • Impedance split: δ depends on how the line impedance is defined, because the even and odd modes' power, voltage and current don't scale identically. The power–voltage definition is the one the 2-D method used. The GSG definition reconstructs each line's signal-against-own-grounds impedance from three eigenmodes, which matches what a GSG launch excites. The power–current value is excluded: in this run the grounds carry 6–11 % more current than the signal, so a total-current definition mixes in the ground currents. New band: δ = 0.21 % to 0.61 %.
  • This band is wider than before (9.3 dB in near-end crosstalk instead of 3.5 dB), which is the honest uncertainty. Narrowing it needs either a 3-D check that discriminates between the definitions, or near-end crosstalk measured on hardware.
  • Caveat: the gold run used the coarser eigenmode mesh (PEC changed by 1 % between coarse and refined), and a single frequency.

4. The model: two lines = an even line + an odd line

Mode tables. Starting from the 3-D line \((\gamma, Z)\) and the split \((\Delta\beta, \delta)\) (2-D shape, gold-corrected, §3.4):

\[ \gamma_{e,o} = \gamma \pm \tfrac{j}{2}\Delta\beta, \qquad Z_{e,o} = Z\left(1 \pm \tfrac{\delta}{2}\right). \]

Each mode is a single uniform line of length \(L\) between \(z_0=50\ \Omega\) ports. With \(\Gamma=(Z-z_0)/(Z+z_0)\) and \(t=e^{-\gamma L}\), summing the multiple reflections gives the formulas of our single-line element:

\[ S_{11}=\frac{\Gamma\,(1-t^2)}{1-\Gamma^2t^2}, \qquad S_{21}=\frac{(1-\Gamma^2)\,t}{1-\Gamma^2t^2}. \]

Recombination. Driving port A1 alone is half an even drive plus half an odd drive. Ports: A1, A2 (line A, near and far end); B1, B2 (line B).

\[ S_{A1A1}=\tfrac12\left(S_{11}^e+S_{11}^o\right),\quad S_{A2A1}=\tfrac12\left(S_{21}^e+S_{21}^o\right),\quad \boxed{\text{NEXT}=S_{B1A1}=\tfrac12\left(S_{11}^e-S_{11}^o\right)},\quad \boxed{\text{FEXT}=S_{B2A1}=\tfrac12\left(S_{21}^e-S_{21}^o\right)} \]

The line voltages recombine the same way, because everything is linear in the voltage: \(V_A(z)=\tfrac12\left[V_e(z)+V_o(z)\right]\) and \(V_B(z)=\tfrac12\left[V_e(z)-V_o(z)\right]\). That's why the voltage the light integrates is also exact for both lines (§6).

5. Length dependence and the shaded band

Nothing is fitted against length. \(L\) enters only through \(t_{e,o}=e^{-\gamma_{e,o}L}\), so a single model covers any length. The physics shows up in two simple limits.

5.1 Far-end crosstalk (FEXT): set by \(\Delta n\), grows with \(L\)

For matched ports (\(\Gamma\approx0\)):

\[ S_{B2A1}=\tfrac12\left(e^{-\gamma_eL}-e^{-\gamma_oL}\right) \;\Rightarrow\; |\text{FEXT}| = e^{-\alpha L}\left|\sin\frac{\Delta\beta\,L}{2}\right| \approx \frac{\pi f L\,\Delta n}{c}\,e^{-\alpha L}. \]

So FEXT grows in proportion to \(f\) and \(L\), reduced by the line loss. For example, at 30 GHz and 14 mm: \(\pi\cdot30\,\text{GHz}\cdot14\,\text{mm}\cdot0.00173/c = 7.6\times10^{-3}\) (−42.4 dB), minus 4.1 dB of loss, gives −46.5 dB. The full model gives −47.0 dB.

5.2 Near-end crosstalk (NEXT): set by \(\delta\), saturates, has nulls

Write the impedances as \(Z_{e,o}=Z(1\pm\delta/2)\). To first order in \(\delta\), and with \(Z\) close to \(z_0\):

\[ \text{NEXT} \approx \frac{\delta}{2}\,Z\frac{\partial S_{11}}{\partial Z} \approx \frac{\delta}{4}\left(1-t^2\right), \qquad |\text{NEXT}|_{\max}\approx\frac{\delta}{2}\;(0.11\text{–}0.31\,\%\Rightarrow -59\text{ to }-50\text{ dB}). \]

NEXT doesn't grow with length. It oscillates between 0 and about \(\delta/2\), and vanishes whenever \(t^2=1\) (for a low-loss line), i.e. \(\beta L=m\pi\):

\[ f_m = \frac{m\,c}{2\,n\,L}\qquad (14\text{ mm}: \approx 5.5\text{ GHz}\cdot m). \]

5.4 The band, precisely

The model is run twice, with \(\delta=0.21\,\%\) and \(\delta=0.61\,\%\) (everything else equal), and the region between the two curves is shaded. It's a deterministic uncertainty envelope, not a statistical spread:
  • NEXT is proportional to δ, so its band is about 9 dB thick.
  • FEXT depends mostly on Δn, so its band is thin.
  • The EO ratio is in between.
Δn is taken as computed (gold-corrected); its range in the GUI title is its variation with frequency, not an uncertainty. INTERCONNECT and the exported tables use the worse case, δ = 0.61 %.

6. The four GUI graphs, one by one

6.1 Panel 1: electrical crosstalk with all ports at 50 Ω

\(|S_{B1A1}|\) (NEXT) and \(|S_{B2A1}|\) (FEXT) of the 4-port, from §4. This is what a VNA would measure on the pair, independent of the real driver and termination. At 14 mm: FEXT −54 / −47 / −43 dB and NEXT −62…−56 / −60…−54 / −56…−52 dB at 10 / 30 / 60 GHz.

6.2 Panel 2: the voltage the light integrates

Circuit: the aggressor A is driven by a 1 V EMF behind the device's source impedance \(Z_s\) at A1. The victim's input B1 ends in its own idle driver (also \(Z_s\)), and both far ends end in the device's termination \(R_t\). Each port's reflection is \(\Gamma_k=(Z_k-z_0)/(Z_k+z_0)\), and the incident waves solve

\[ \mathbf a = (\mathbf I - \mathbf G\,\mathbf S)^{-1}\,\mathbf a_{\rm src}, \quad \mathbf G=\mathrm{diag}(\Gamma_s,\Gamma_t,\Gamma_s,\Gamma_t), \quad \mathbf a_{\rm src}=\left(\tfrac{z_0}{Z_s+z_0},0,0,0\right). \]

What the light sees: the optical wave travels at the group velocity \(c/n_g\), so a phase shifter integrates the line voltage in a frame moving with the light:

\[ H_k(f)=\frac{1}{L}\int_0^L V_k(z)\,e^{\,j\beta_o(z-L)}\,dz,\qquad \beta_o=\frac{2\pi f\,n_g}{c},\qquad k=A,B. \]

Per mode, \(V(z)=V^+e^{-\gamma z}+V^-e^{+\gamma z}\), so the integral is closed-form, with \(\int_0^L e^{qz}dz=(e^{qL}-1)/q\) for \(q=\mp\gamma+j\beta_o\). The modes are then recombined as \(H_{A,B}=\tfrac12(H_e\pm H_o)\). This is exactly the single-line element's "modulation" output, evaluated once per mode. The panel shows \(|H_A|\) and \(|H_B|\), relative to \(|H_A|\) at the lowest frequency.

6.3 Panel 3: EO crosstalk

\[ \text{EO crosstalk}(f) = \left|\frac{H_B(f)}{H_A(f)}\right|. \]

This is the victim's phase modulation relative to the aggressor's, at each frequency, in the real circuit. It answers the question "how much of the aggressor's signal appears on the neighbouring modulator's optical output". At 14 mm it is about −55 / −47 / −36 dB at 10 / 30 / 60 GHz (band ±1–2 dB).

Why it rises to −37 dB at 60 GHz

It's mostly the denominator. The aggressor's own response has a walk-off null where \(f\,|n_g-n|\,L/c\approx1\): with \(n_g=2.27\) and \(n\approx1.93\), that's \(L\approx c/(60\,\text{GHz}\cdot0.34)\approx14.7\) mm. Panel 2 shows \(|H_A|\) falling by about 20 dB at 60 GHz. The victim's response, a difference of the even and odd terms, doesn't null at the same place, so the ratio peaks. In absolute terms (panel 2), the victim stays around −55 to −60 dB relative to the aggressor's low-frequency response.

6.4 Panel 4: the victim's terminal voltages

\(|V_B(0)/V_{A1}|\) (near end) and \(|V_B(L)/V_{A1}|\) (far end), in the same circuit as panel 2, relative to the voltage actually present at the aggressor's input. They are panel 1's quantities with the real driver and termination: what would appear at the neighbour's driver output and at its termination resistor.

7. The INTERCONNECT adaptation

7.1 From the single-line element to the "Coupled TL" element

The single-line travelling-wave element (tl_element_setup.lsf) reads one table (frequency, loss, index, Z). It sets its 2-port S-matrix with the formulas of §4, and computes its "modulation" output from the exact \(V(z)\) integral of §6.2. The new element (coupled_element_setup.lsf) does the same computation twice, from coupled_even.txt and coupled_odd.txt, and recombines:

  • 4 electrical ports A1, A2, B1, B2: the 16 S-parameters as \(\tfrac12(\text{even}\pm\text{odd})\);
  • modulation A / B: the light's voltage on each line (optionally modulation 2 A / B at a second arm's ng);
  • optional far end A / B and near end A / B: the terminal voltages.

It is an ordinary scripted element, just like the single-line one: no segmentation, valid for any length (a property), and its tables come from the GUI's "Export tables" button.

7.2 What PAIR_MOD, PAIR_FE and PAIR_NE are

They are the acceptance test bench built by the "INTERCONNECT check" button (xtalk_test.py), not a device model. Each row is the circuit of §6.2, built around the Coupled TL element:

  • SRC_*: the scripted TL source, the driver with impedance \(Z_s\). The ENA's output drives it.
  • CTL_*: the Coupled TL element under test.
  • LOAD_B1_*: \(Z_s\), the victim's idle driver at its input.
  • LOAD_A2_*, LOAD_B2_*: \(R_t\), both far-end terminations.
  • RELAY_*: the compound's boundary ports, created automatically.

Why a Compound: our Step 0 tests showed that INTERCONNECT computes reflections between blocks exactly only inside a Compound with scattering-data analysis on.

Why three rows: an analyser reads two outputs at a time, and a Compound may not leave an output unconnected. So each row creates only the outputs it reads: MOD (modulation A/B), FE (far end A/B) and NE (near end A/B).

The check then compares each trace with the twin, as described in §2.

7.3 Why it stops at the ENA, and not in the full cascade

The test bench isolates the element so that any disagreement can only come from the element. The full-cascade build in crosstalk mode was deliberately left for later: we agreed to validate the element first and not modify the MZM build code yet. The crosstalk work then moved to the slow-wave (Istanbul) line. So nothing blocks it technically. It just hasn't been done.

8. Should it go into the full MZM cascade?

Update: now implemented (straight electrode)

The Crosstalk window has two new buttons: "Full MZM cascade in INTERCONNECT: ENA + check" and "…: eyes". They build both modulators (the sidebar's optics, bias and drive) on one Coupled TL electrode. ENA input 1 reads modulator A's photodiode and input 2 modulator B's, and their ratio is checked against the twin. In eye mode, EYE_1 is A and EYE_2 is what reaches B with its driver idle. Code: mzm_interconnect/xtalk_cascade.py. The build logic was checked offline against a mock of INTERCONNECT; the first real run is still to be done on your machine.

Yes. It's the natural form for presenting and using the model, and it's mechanically straightforward, because the Coupled TL ports are ordinary electrical ports, like the single-line element's. In the cascade, only the section where the two electrodes run parallel is coupled. Everything else stays as it is today:

  • Electrical: each modulator keeps its own chain: driver, pads and feed, curves, exit, termination. The two straight sections are replaced by one Coupled TL. B gets its own driver: idle (\(Z_s\)) to see pure crosstalk, or its own PRBS to see the combined effect.
  • Optical: modulation A and modulation 2 A drive modulator A's two arms, as the single-line element does today. modulation B and modulation 2 B drive the neighbour's arms.
  • What you gain: EO crosstalk shows up where it matters, in the victim's optical output. That means the eye opening, extinction and BER penalty of modulator B while A carries data, the curves and pads included, rather than only as a ratio of transfer functions.
  • Effort: moderate. The build code already assembles the single-line cascade inside a Compound with scattering analysis, and the Coupled TL element and its tables exist. What's needed: a "crosstalk" branch in the builder (the GUI's existing "Model" selector) that duplicates the electrical chain for B, swaps the straight section for the coupled element, and adds modulator B's optics. Then one comparison against the twin, extended with the single-line elements on either side.
  • Caveat: the coupling is modelled only along the parallel section. In the curves and pads the lines separate, and their coupling there, which the 3-D Istanbul runs suggest can be significant at the ports, is not included (§9).

Appendix A. How δ relates to Δn

They are not derived from each other. Both come from the two coupling coefficients of the pair. Each line has a self inductance and capacitance per unit length, \(L'\) and \(C'\); the lines are coupled by a mutual inductance and a mutual capacitance:

  • Even mode (both signals at the same voltage, currents in the same direction): the mutual inductance adds, and with no voltage between the lines, the mutual capacitance carries no charge.
  • Odd mode: the mutual inductance subtracts, and the full voltage difference appears across the mutual capacitance.

With small inductive and capacitive coupling coefficients \(k_L\) and \(k_C\):

\[ L'_{e}=L'(1+k_L),\quad L'_{o}=L'(1-k_L),\qquad C'_{e}=C'(1-k_C),\quad C'_{o}=C'(1+k_C). \]

Each mode is an ordinary line, with \(Z=\sqrt{L'/C'}\) and \(n=c\sqrt{L'C'}\). To first order:

\[ \delta=\frac{Z_e}{Z_o}-1\approx k_L+k_C,\qquad \frac{\Delta n}{n}=\frac{n_e-n_o}{n}\approx k_L-k_C . \]
δ is the sum of the inductive and capacitive couplings, and Δn/n is their difference. In a homogeneous dielectric (pure TEM), \(k_L=k_C\), so Δn = 0 (no far-end crosstalk) while δ = 2k ≠ 0. On LN/SiO₂/Si with air above, \(k_L\neq k_C\). With \(\Delta n/n=0.00173/1.95=0.089\,\%\) and δ ≈ 0.4 %: \(k_L\approx0.24\,\%\) and \(k_C\approx0.16\,\%\).

Appendix B. How Δn and δ were extracted; impedance definitions

B.1 Index split Δn

  1. 2-D port solve (PEC metal). CST solves the cross-section as an eigenvalue problem and reports each mode's propagation constant β ("Beta" in the port info box). Mode 5 is even and mode 6 odd (Appendix E), so \(\Delta n=(\beta_5-\beta_6)\,c/(2\pi f)\): 0.00151 at 30 GHz.
  2. Gold correction (eigenmode solver). One 10 µm slice of the pair, periodic boundaries with a fixed phase advance φ, so β = φ/p is fixed. The solver returns each mode's frequency: the even mode with a magnetic wall at the midplane, the odd mode with an electric wall. With the same β, \(n=c\varphi/(2\pi f p)\), so \(\Delta n/n=(f_o-f_e)/f\). Gold: \(f_o-f_e=25.5\) MHz at 28.89 GHz, which gives Δn = 0.00173 (PEC, same mesh: 0.00145). The model keeps the 2-D frequency shape, scaled by 0.00173/0.00151 = 1.146.

B.2 Impedance split δ and the definitions

For each mode: V = signal potential minus the mean of that line's two ground potentials (integral of E across the strips); I = signal current (line integral of H around the signal strip); P = power flow (Poynting vector integrated over the cross-section).

B.3 Why the definitions don't coincide: physics or artefact?

Mostly physics, with a contribution from the eigenmode set-up. Not the mesh.

  1. Intrinsic (physical). \(Z_{PV}=Z_{VI}=Z_{PI}\) holds exactly only for a lossless TEM line, where \(P=\tfrac12 VI^*\) and the voltage between two conductors doesn't depend on the integration path. Ours isn't TEM:
    • the dielectric is inhomogeneous, so there are small longitudinal fields and the line voltage depends slightly on the path;
    • gold is lossy: the field penetrates the metal and the current redistributes, so V, I and P are no longer in fixed ratios, and Z is complex.
    The definitions then differ by small relative amounts, which are the same order as δ itself, since δ is a 0.2–0.6 % difference between two nearly equal impedances. The same is known for microstrip, where the three definitions differ at the percent level.
  2. Multi-conductor current sharing (physical, but set-up dependent). In the eigenmode run, the coplanar mode isn't a pure three-conductor GSG mode. The grounds carry 6–11 % more current than the signal, and they sit at −0.26 to −0.30 of the signal's potential relative to the enclosure, so part of the return current flows in the enclosure walls. The power–current and voltage–current definitions take the signal current alone, so they are the most affected. That's why the GSG reconstruction, which enforces "signal current returns on its own grounds", is the physically closest to a probe launch. The enclosure is a property of the simulation set-up, so this part is set-up dependent.
  3. Not the mesh. Refining the eigenmode mesh (64.5k → 168k cells) changed the power–voltage δ by 2 % of its value (0.539 → 0.528 %) and Δn by 1 %, far below the 0.2-vs-0.6 % spread.

Which one is right for our S-parameters? The one consistent with how the port defines voltage and current. A GSG probe or the 3-D bridge port imposes the signal against its own tied grounds, so the GSG value (+0.21 %) is the natural choice. The reliable 3-D data agree: fitting δ to the coworker's 1 mm 3-D NEXT (error floor −73 dB), 5–50 GHz, with the gold Δn, gives δ = 0.23 % (rms 1.9 dB); δ = 0.61 % would be 8.7 dB too high. The 5 mm run can't decide (its NEXT sits on its error floor; no δ fits better than 9 dB rms). So the evidence points to the lower edge of the band. The band is kept until a second reliable 3-D length confirms it.

Appendix C. Why δ drives NEXT and Δn drives FEXT (derivation)

Single line between \(z_0\) ports, with \(\Gamma=(Z-z_0)/(Z+z_0)\) and \(t=e^{-\gamma L}\):

\[ S_{11}=\frac{\Gamma(1-t^2)}{1-\Gamma^2t^2},\qquad S_{21}=\frac{(1-\Gamma^2)\,t}{1-\Gamma^2t^2},\qquad \text{NEXT}=\tfrac12(S_{11}^e-S_{11}^o),\quad \text{FEXT}=\tfrac12(S_{21}^e-S_{21}^o). \]

The two modes differ by \(\Delta Z=Z\delta\) and \(\Delta\gamma=j\Delta\beta\), so to first order every even−odd difference is

\[ X^e-X^o\approx\frac{\partial X}{\partial Z}\,Z\delta+\frac{\partial X}{\partial \gamma}\,j\Delta\beta . \]

NEXT

  • δ term. \(\partial S_{11}/\partial\Gamma\approx1-t^2\), and \(\partial\Gamma/\partial Z=2z_0/(Z+z_0)^2\approx1/(2Z)\). So \[ \text{NEXT}_\delta\approx\frac{\delta}{4}(1-t^2), \] first order in δ, with no small prefactor.
  • Δn term. \(\partial S_{11}/\partial t\propto-2\Gamma t\), so \(\text{NEXT}_{\Delta n}\propto\Gamma\,\Delta\beta L\,t^2\). This is far-end crosstalk reflected back by the mismatch (Γ ≈ −0.16 for 36.6 Ω in 50 Ω). It is small, and zero for a matched line.

So NEXT ∝ δ. A factor 0.61/0.21 in δ gives \(20\log_{10}(0.61/0.21)=9.3\) dB.

FEXT

  • Δn term. \(\partial S_{21}/\partial t\approx1-\Gamma^2\approx1\), and \(t_e-t_o=-tL\,j\Delta\beta\). So \[ \text{FEXT}_{\Delta n}\approx-\tfrac{j}{2}\,t\,\Delta\beta L\;\Rightarrow\;|\text{FEXT}|\approx e^{-\alpha L}\frac{\pi f L\,\Delta n}{c}, \] first order in Δn.
  • δ term. \(\partial S_{21}/\partial\Gamma\approx-2\Gamma t(1-t^2)\), so \(\text{FEXT}_\delta\approx-\Gamma\,\delta\,t(1-t^2)\): second order (Γ × δ).

At 14 mm and 30 GHz: \(\Delta\beta L/2=7.6\times10^{-3}\) (−42 dB), while \(|\Gamma|\delta\lesssim1\times10^{-3}\) (≤ −60 dB). So δ barely moves FEXT. At 1 mm the Δn term is 14 times smaller and the δ term becomes visible, which is why the FEXT band is wider at short lengths.

Physical picture (Appendix A): backward coupling adds the inductive and capacitive parts (\(k_L+k_C\approx\delta\)), and forward coupling subtracts them (\(k_L-k_C\approx\Delta n/n\)). It is the classic coupled-line result.

Appendix D. The average line from the 3-D runs, step by step

  1. Even and odd 2-ports from a 4-port. Each 3-D run gives a 4×4 S-matrix (A1, A2, B1, B2). Driving A1 and B1 with equal, in-phase waves is the even mode; with opposite signs, the odd mode. By symmetry, each behaves as a 2-port (near end → far end): \[ S^{e,o}_{11}=S_{A1A1}\pm S_{A1B1},\qquad S^{e,o}_{21}=S_{A2A1}\pm S_{A2B1}. \]
  2. Two lengths remove the ports. Each measured 2-port is port transition × line × mirrored transition. In transfer matrices, \(T_L=P\,M(L)\,\tilde P\), with the same P in the 1 mm and 5 mm runs. Then \(T_5T_1^{-1}=P\,M(4\,\text{mm})\,P^{-1}\), whose eigenvalues are \(e^{\pm\gamma\cdot4\,\text{mm}}\): γ = α + jβ, exactly, with no port model.
  3. Impedance. Each transition is modelled as a small series impedance plus a shunt admittance, fitted together with Z to both lengths' S-parameters.
  4. Average. \(\gamma=\tfrac12(\gamma_e+\gamma_o)\) and \(Z=\tfrac12(Z_e+Z_o)\) give the isolated line to first order. The even/odd difference itself is below these runs' numerical noise, so only the average is used.
  5. Cross-check. Within 0.6 % (index) and 3.6 % (impedance) of the coworker's separate single-line 3-D runs (§3.1).

Appendix E. How modes 5 and 6 were identified

Not by eye. The identification uses four independent criteria, and modes 5 and 6 pass all four:

  1. Count. 6 strips + the port's metal frame = 7 conductors, so exactly 6 quasi-TEM modes. CST returned 6, so none is missing, and any further mode would have a cutoff.
  2. Symmetry under A ↔ B (mirror plane between the lines). Every mode is either even or odd. For modes 5 and 6 this was checked numerically from the exported fields: integrating \(E_y\) gives each strip's potential, and \(V_A\approx+V_B\) for mode 5 and \(V_A\approx-V_B\) for mode 6. They agree within about 8 %, the residual mixing of two nearly degenerate modes, which the power-based δ formula absorbs.
  3. Symmetry of each line about its own signal (the coplanar mode). In modes 5 and 6, each line's two grounds are at equal potential and the signal is opposite them, so each line's net current is zero (the signal returns on its own grounds). That is exactly what a GSG launch excites (§3.3). In contrast:
    • in modes 3 and 4, a line's two grounds are at opposite potentials (slot modes);
    • in modes 1 and 2, the whole line (signal and grounds) is at one potential against the frame, with net current returning through the frame.
  4. Index and impedance.
    • Modes 5 and 6 are a near-degenerate pair (n = 1.8878 and 1.8863, Δn = 1.5 × 10⁻³), as two weakly coupled identical lines must be, and at the coplanar index.
    • Modes 1–4 have n = 2.06–2.41: more field in the Si, which modes involving the grounds or the frame have.
    • CST's line impedance for modes 5 and 6 (17.4 and 17.2 Ω, total over both lines) is half of a single coplanar line's (~35 Ω): two lines in parallel, as expected.

The field pictures (§3.3) are consistent with all of this, but the assignment rests on criteria 2–4.
